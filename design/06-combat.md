# 6. Combat

Combat is rare, fast, chaotic and expensive: most shifts have none, a shooting lasts three or four turns, and every round fired ends up in a report, a review, and possibly your nightmares.

## Critical Time

Exploration runs in real-time-with-pause. A dangerous moment drops the game into Critical Time:

- One turn ≈ 1.5 seconds.
- 2 Action Points (AP) per turn, plus one free short verbal command ("Drop it!").
- Turn order from Reaction (Perception + Nerve); surprise costs your first turn.
- Habits, stress effects and trauma episodes all apply (section 5).

## Actions

| Action | AP | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Move (short) / run | 1 / 2 | About 3–4 m per point |
| Draw weapon | 1 | Drawing without justification triggers review |
| Aim | 1 | +20 to next shot |
| Fire single / pair | 1 / 2 | Second shot of a pair −10 |
| Hold aim (cover stance) | 2 | Reaction shot if the target makes a threatening move |
| Take cover | 1 | |
| Reload | 1–2 | Fumbled reloads under stress cost more |
| Clear malfunction | 1–2 | Composure check under fire |
| Longer verbal command | 1 | First short command each turn is free |
| Radio | 1 | Clear transmission needs a check when stressed |
| Hands-on step | 1–2 | Grab, takedown, cuffing step |
| Less-lethal | 1 | |
| First aid | 2 | A tourniquet is a full turn |
| Scan | 1 | Reveals what tunnel vision hides |

**Action beats reaction.** A suspect who has decided to draw is faster than you can react unless you are already in cover stance. That is why officers draw early, and why drawing early is scrutinized.

**Threat recognition.** When a hand comes out of a pocket or waistband, a Perception check identifies the object: gun, phone, wallet or knife. Darkness, distance, low Composure and triggers raise the chance of misidentification. The game always knows what was really in the hand.

## Firearms

```
Hit = (Perception × 5) + (Firearms × 10) + aim − stress − movement − cover − light − range
```

| Range band | Distance | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Contact | 0–1 m | Weapon retention matters; messy |
| Close | 1–7 m | Where most police shootings happen |
| Medium | 7–15 m | Hits drop sharply under stress |
| Long | 15 m+ | Rare for pistols; shotgun slugs work |

Typical modifiers: stress up to −30, you moving −20, target moving −15, darkness without light −20, partial cover −20, aim +20. Under real stress most rounds miss, as in real police shootings.

**Every round goes somewhere.** Rampart is apartments, cars, windows and children. Misses are tracked and can penetrate walls or hit bystanders; a Perception check before firing shows what's behind the target, if you take the time. Every round fired becomes a memory card, and "How many shots did you fire, Officer?" is a guaranteed question later.

**Malfunctions:** a small chance per shot, higher with bad kit-room gear or a weapon you didn't clean.

## Wounds and incapacitation

- **Hit locations:** center mass, limbs, head (a heavy penalty and very hard to justify).
- **People don't just drop.** Fear and adrenaline keep a wounded suspect fighting for turns; Spark or booster users absorb far more.
- **Bleeding** continues each turn until treated; tourniquets and pressure save lives, including the suspect's.
- **Your vest** turns many handgun hits into bruised ribs and a lost turn of breath.

## Less-lethal and hands-on

- **Taser:** both probes must hit; thick clothing defeats it; some boosted suspects shrug it off.
- **Beanbag:** needs distance; can still kill at close range or with a bad shot.
- **OC spray:** fills small rooms, including your lungs.
- **Hands-on:** a grapple, control and cuff sequence using Defensive Tactics and Fitness; losing a struggle invites a gun grab, so retention checks matter. Cuffing a resisting suspect is one of the most dangerous moments in any fight.

## Suspect behavior

Each suspect has a **disposition** that can change every turn (compliant, passive resistance, active resistance, assaultive, deadly) and a **state** (panicked, high, mentally ill, drunk, gang-loyal, afraid of deportation, combat-trained). Commands, distance and patience can lower disposition; rushing in raises it.

## Partner tactics and backup

- **Contact and cover:** one officer talks and handles, the other watches and covers. Mixing roles is dangerous.
- **Partner requests:** flank, cover, hold, talk, cuff; whether they comply depends on personality and approval.
- **Crossfire** is shown and real.
- **Backup:** arrives in turns based on distance and how busy the division is. "Officer needs help" brings everyone, fast and chaotic, and arriving units can escalate. An airship arrives for night pursuits and perimeters.

## Pursuits

- **Foot:** Athletics and Fitness against the suspect; fences, alleys, dogs. Chasing alone into a dark yard is how cops die, and your FTO will tell you so, if you live.
- **Vehicle:** top-down driving through the real grid, with traffic and pedestrians. A supervisor can terminate the pursuit; ignoring that ends careers, and a crash that kills a civilian ends more.

## Cape incidents

- **Tier 3+:** survival only. Evacuate, triage, set a perimeter, avoid collapsing buildings. Shooting at them is useless or draws attention.
- **Tier 1–2:** conventional tools sometimes work. A Street-level brute may shrug off a Taser and fall to a shotgun slug.
- **Debris hazards:** collapsing walls, burning cars, downed lines, falling glass, telegraphed on the map.
- **Federal takeover:** agency teams with suppression gear arrive and push you aside.

## After the shooting stops

1. Secure weapons, treat the wounded (including the suspect), call for medical help, preserve the scene.
2. For an officer-involved shooting: you're separated from your partner, your weapon is taken as evidence, you see the department psychologist, and a long investigation begins while you sit on desk duty.
3. Memory cards form from the incident; some are wrong (section 9).

## Use-of-force evaluation

Every application of force is judged on the threat at that moment (as it really was and as you perceived it), proportionality, warnings and de-escalation attempts, the tactics that led there, and your report and testimony.

| Outcome | Result |
|---|---|
| In policy | No action |
| In policy, tactical debrief | Retraining; FTO note |
| Administrative disapproval | Discipline, file note, possible suspension |
| Out of policy | Firing, possible criminal referral |

A shooting can be legal and still out of policy, or in policy and still the wrong call, and the game makes sure you know it.

## Example: Pico-Union stairwell

A man high on Spark with a kitchen knife on a third-floor landing; a child crying behind a door; your FTO two flights down. De-escalation 40%, Taser 55% (Spark resists it), a shot 60% in a narrow stairwell where a miss goes through the family's wall. Every outcome ends with a report, an evaluation, and a question in court.
