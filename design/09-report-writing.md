# 9. Report writing

Reports are where the game's honesty system lives: everything you do becomes a written record that follows you into court, review boards, Ledger hearings and your career. Reports are paper forms on a clipboard, mostly handwritten, as in 2010.

## Report types

| Report | When | What it decides |
|---|---|---|
| Investigative (crime) report | Any crime | Whether detectives and the DA can act |
| Arrest report | Every arrest | Whether charges are filed and survive |
| Use-of-force statement | Any force used | Whether the force was in policy |
| Field interview card | Stops and contacts | Intelligence later; profiling data under the consent decree |
| Daily field activities log | Every shift | Numbers and accountability |
| Ledger incident statement (world) | Cape incidents | Evidence review boards use to assign deaths |

## The interface

Three panels:

1. **Memory:** what your character remembers, as fact cards ("Suspect reached toward waistband", "Arrival about 22:14").
2. **Sources:** your notebook, witness statements, your partner's notes, evidence photos, civilian phone video if anyone hands it over.
3. **Draft:** the form's sections (how the call came in, what you found, statements, evidence, actions, disposition). You place fact cards, order them, and choose wording.

## Memory under stress

Cards formed at low Composure may be:

- **Clear:** accurate.
- **Fuzzy:** marked uncertain; partly right.
- **Distorted:** feels certain, is wrong, and looks like a normal card until another source contradicts it.

The dilemma is real: write what you remember and risk being wrong, or write "I don't recall" and look weak. Corroboration (partner's notes, a witness, physical evidence) can clarify a card before you commit it.

## Wording

| Wording | Example | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Precise | "He moved his right hand toward his right front waistband, where I saw a bulge" | Strongest in court; slowest; needs a Report Writing check |
| Boilerplate | "Suspect made a furtive movement" | Fast; weak under cross; a red flag for IA |
| Slanted | "He aggressively reached for what appeared to be a weapon" | Protects you if it holds; dangerous if contradicted |
| False | Something that didn't happen | Fixes today; perjury if exposed |
| Omission | A card left out | Not a lie, until it's shown to be deliberate |

Report Writing unlocks precise wording and saves time; Intellect sets how many cards you can manage.

## Required elements

Each report type has legal elements (robbery needs force or fear and property taken; a search needs a justification; a confession needs a proper Miranda warning). A successful Law and Policy check flags missing elements while you write. Fail it and you find out when the DA rejects the case, a judge suppresses the evidence, or a guilty suspect walks.

## Habits in writing

- **Military:** "engaged", "neutralized", "hostile"; defense attorneys love it.
- **Cadet program:** clean, by the book, full of boilerplate.
- **College graduate:** thorough, precise, slow; overtime.
- **Cape industry:** frames the story the way a PR person would.

## The consistency engine

The game tracks (1) what actually happened, hidden; (2) what your report says; (3) what every other source says: your partner's report, witnesses, evidence, medical records, video. Each contradiction becomes a flag that can surface weeks later. Honest errors cost little; caught lies cost a lot.

## Pressure

- **Time:** end of watch, tired; careful reports mean overtime, rushed ones mean risk; your sergeant can kick a report back.
- **Partners:** comparing notes on routine calls is normal and improves accuracy; getting your stories straight after a use of force is collusion, possible, and career-ending if discovered.
- **Outsiders:** a hero's lawyer's voicemail, a sergeant who wants it softened, an FTO who asks "you didn't see the feds, right?"
- **FTO style:** Ortega demands precision and will not tolerate lies, and also teaches where omissions hide; Whitfield teaches how to write a shooting that survives review; Arakawa asks you to leave out something small that isn't.

## Downstream

| Stage | Checks |
|---|---|
| DA filing | Elements and clarity; rejection releases the suspect |
| Court | Contradictions and boilerplate under cross (section 10) |
| Use-of-force review | Whether your account justifies the force |
| Internal Affairs | Complaints matched to your reports |
| Ledger review board | Who is blamed for deaths |
| Civil lawsuit | Old reports read aloud in a deposition, years later |

## Credibility and the Brady list

Each proven inconsistency lowers a hidden Credibility score. Fall far enough and prosecutors put you on their list of officers with credibility problems (a Brady list): your testimony becomes a liability, your arrests stop sticking, and your working career is over. Some dirty cops in the game are already on it, which is why the gang unit wants clean rookies.

## Example: the Héctor Ruiz debris death

Your cards from the 6th Street fight:

- The hero struck the "villain", who barely reacted (fuzzy: you were triaging).
- Héctor Ruiz was crushed in his car by debris (clear).
- Castellan's PR rep arrived ten minutes after the fight, before the coroner (clear).
- Federal agents took two uninjured people from the Hole (clear, from a distance).
- A Restora truck was parked on the block before the fight began (distorted?).

Options: write everything precisely (honest, and now every faction knows your name); leave out the agents (the handler agency remembers the favor; the missing-persons thread gets harder); use Castellan's suggested "unavoidable debris during a legitimate engagement" (a powerful friend and a guilty conscience); include the truck (a key clue if true, and if your memory is distorted, the lever Castellan's lawyers use to destroy the whole report). This report shapes how Act 2 opens.
